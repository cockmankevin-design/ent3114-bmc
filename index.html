Thesis

Nine boxes. Nine bets. A first-draft Business Model Canvas for a venture that teaches young adults to invest by making them put real money down and write why — and the list of assumptions underneath it that could each be wrong.

Kevin Cockman BMC & Key Assumptions Assignment Prof. Mark Bole August 31, 2026

01 — Carried forward

The problem this canvas is built on

Every cell below is downstream of this statement. If the root cause is wrong, the canvas is wrong, and no amount of tidy boxes fixes it.

Problem statement

Young adults with their first real income never start investing not because they lack access to financial information, but because every resource available to them teaches without stakes — and nothing about money sticks until a decision has a consequence you feel.

Root cause

Financial education is delivered as content. Content without consequence does not change behavior. A student can watch forty hours of investing videos, pass a quiz, and still have a brokerage app on their phone they have never funded.

The gap is not knowledge. It is the absence of a first decision that costs something.

My unique insight (and why I believe it)

The people who need this most are not uninformed. They can already explain what an index fund is and still have never opened an account. That single observation kills the "more education" solution that almost everyone else builds.

I believe it because it describes how I learned what I actually know about money: not from a course, but from running things where being wrong cost me something real.

02 — The canvas

Nine boxes. Nine bets.

Each block is a category of bet, not a description. Every cell below looks reasonable. Every cell could also be wrong — which is the entire point of section 03.

First draft. This is a living document and it updates every time an interview tells me something real.

Key Partners

Who I bet will help me.

  • Brokerage-as-a-service provider (Alpaca, DriveWealth, Apex) — custody, fractional shares, KYC, the licence I do not have
  • FGCU Daveler & Kauanui School and campus business orgs — first cohort, free distribution
  • A securities attorney — to confirm the introducing-app structure before launch, not after
  • Credit unions and first-job employers — later sponsorship of cohorts

Key Activities

What I bet I'll do.

  • Run the weekly loop: one decision prompt, a written thesis, a trade window, a scored review
  • Write the weekly one-decision brief
  • Onboarding and KYC handoff to the broker partner
  • Recruit and retain campus cohorts

Key Resources

What I bet I'll need.

  • An approved broker-partner integration
  • The thesis rubric — how a decision gets scored on reasoning rather than return. This is the actual product
  • Access to FGCU students and student orgs
  • My own build stack — single-file web app, no engineering payroll

Value Propositions

What I bet they'll want.

You don't learn to invest by watching. You learn by putting $10 down and writing why.

  • One real decision a week, with real money, in amounts small enough that being wrong does not hurt
  • You must write a thesis before you can buy — and you are scored on the thinking, not the return
  • Not a course: a course removes the stakes
  • Not a robo-advisor: a robo-advisor removes the decision, which is the only part that teaches

Customer Relationships

How I bet I'll keep them.

  • A weekly cohort of 20–30 students moving through the same decision together
  • A shared thesis feed inside the cohort — accountability without a leaderboard
  • A scored review after each decision: was the thesis right, and right for the right reason?
  • Deliberately no streaks and no rewards for trading more often

Channels

How I bet I'll reach them.

  • Campus organisations — investment clubs, business fraternities, the D&K School
  • Professor referrals in intro finance and entrepreneurship courses
  • Short-form video: real theses, real outcomes, small dollars
  • Cohort invites — each member brings two

Customer Segments

Who I bet will buy.

College students 18–24 with part-time income or a loan refund, who can already explain what an index fund is and still have never funded a brokerage account.

  • Marcus, 20, FGCU junior, works 18 hrs/week, ~$600/month. Installed a trading app a year ago. Has never put money in it.
  • Not high-schoolers — no income, so no stakes are possible
  • Not 35+ — a different pain entirely (consolidation, not initiation)

Cost Structure

What I bet it'll cost.

  • Brokerage-as-a-service platform fee plus a per-funded-account cost
  • Legal review of the introducing-app structure, terms and disclosures — the largest pre-launch line
  • KYC and compliance pass-through per account
  • Paid acquisition tests at $20–50 a round
  • My time. No salaries while this is one person and one HTML file

Revenue Streams

How much I bet they'll pay.

  • $5/month student subscription, or $40/year
  • Institutional licence — a school or club buys a block of cohort seats
  • Later: employer or credit-union sponsorship of a cohort
  • Explicitly not payment for order flow, and nothing that pays me more when a student trades more. That incentive would destroy the product

03 — The one that matters

Leap of faith assumption

Of everything on this canvas, this is the bet that ends the business if it comes back wrong. Not "makes it harder" — ends it.

Leap of faith

We believe college students aged 18–24 with part-time income will link a real bank account and fund at least $25 into a student-built investing app within seven days of signing up.

Why it kills the business

Everything here rests on the money being real. Stakes are the entire mechanism — the reason Thesis is not just another free course. If students will only paper-trade, the stakes vanish, the product collapses into content, and content is the thing that already exists for free and already does not work.

How I'd know within two weeks

A smoke test. A landing page that explains the offer and asks for a $25 deposit at the end of signup. Drive 300 students to it through campus orgs and $50 of ads. Measure the funding rate, not the email rate. Under 10% funded and the premise is wrong. No product code gets written before that number exists.

04 — The rest of the bets

Key assumptions worth validating

Ordered by what I would test first: cheapest and most fatal at the top. Each one is specific enough that I can picture the experiment that proves it wrong — if I couldn't, it isn't on this list.

01Key PartnersRegulatory

We believe a student-founded app can operate on a licensed partner's rails — taking deposits, placing fractional trades — without itself registering as a broker-dealer or an investment adviser.

How I test it

One conversation with a securities attorney, plus the compliance packet from two brokerage-as-a-service providers. A binary answer, available this week. Cost: one consult · Time: 1 week

What would disprove it

Any structure that requires my own registration, or a partner minimum a pre-revenue student cannot meet.

02Value Propositions

We believe the binding constraint is stakes, not information — that students who can already explain an index fund have still never funded a brokerage account.

How I test it

15 interviews. Ask two separate things: what they can explain, and what they have actually done. Don't pitch. Listen. Cost: $0 · Time: this week

What would disprove it

If most students who haven't invested also can't explain the basics, the problem is information after all — and the insight this venture is built on is wrong.

03Revenue Streams

We believe a student who will fund $25 will also pay $5 a month for the weekly decision loop, rather than only using a free tier.

How I test it

Put a real checkout on the smoke-test page. Charge before anything is built, refund everyone afterwards. Count cards, not clicks. Cost: payment fees · Time: 1 week

What would disprove it

Fewer than 5% of funded signups reaching for a card. That pushes the model toward institutional licensing and rewrites the revenue block.

04Value PropositionsMechanism

We believe forcing a written thesis before a student can buy raises 30-day retention rather than causing them to abandon signup.

How I test it

Two onboarding flows run side by side in a concierge cohort: thesis required versus one-tap buy. Same prompt, same week, 20 students each. Cost: my time · Time: 4 weeks

What would disprove it

The thesis group dropping off faster. The friction I am selling would be friction they won't accept, and the mechanism needs to get lighter.

05Channels

We believe campus organisations will distribute this to their members for free, because running it makes the organisation look useful to its own members.

How I test it

Pitch five student-org presidents at FGCU. Count how many agree to email their list or run one session. Ask for the commitment inside the meeting. Cost: $0 · Time: 2 weeks

What would disprove it

Fewer than two of five saying yes. Free campus distribution is what makes $5/month work; without it, paid acquisition has to carry the whole model.

06Customer Segments

We believe students 18–24 with a first part-time income feel the "I should be investing and I'm not" pressure sharply enough to act on it — more sharply than high-schoolers or adults over 30.

How I test it

Five interviews in each of three age bands, identical questions. Count how many describe the gap unprompted, before I name it for them. Cost: $0 · Time: this week

What would disprove it

The pressure showing up strongest in a different band. That is a segment change rather than a failure — but channels, price and persona all move with it.

07Customer Relationships

We believe a weekly cohort of 20–30 students retains better than the same product used alone.

How I test it

Concierge it. Run a cohort manually for four weeks with a group chat and a spreadsheet — no product at all, just me. Track how many still post a thesis in week four. Cost: my time · Time: 4 weeks

What would disprove it

Week-four participation under 40%. Cohorts are expensive to run; if they don't hold people, the relationships block is wrong.

08Cost Structure

We believe a funded student can be acquired for under $15 through campus organisations and short-form video.

How I test it

$50 of ads to the smoke-test page. Divide spend by funded accounts, not by email signups. Cost: $50 · Time: 3 days

What would disprove it

Cost per funded account above $15 against $60 of annual revenue. Payback stretches past a student's remaining time on campus, and the institutional licence becomes the only model.
